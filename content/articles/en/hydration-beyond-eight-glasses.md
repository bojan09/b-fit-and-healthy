"Drink eight glasses of water a day" is easy to remember, but it was never based on a precise measurement of individual need — it is a rounded, one-size guess that has stuck around mostly because it is simple to repeat. Hydration needs shift with body size, activity level, climate, and even the water content of the food you eat, so one fixed number cannot fit everyone equally well.

## Why the rule falls short

A larger, more active person training outdoors in summer heat needs noticeably more fluid than someone smaller and sedentary in a mild climate, and no single glass count can bridge that gap. Food also contributes meaningfully to daily fluid intake — soups, fruit, and vegetables all supply water alongside whatever you drink from a glass or bottle. Treating eight glasses as a strict quota ignores all of this ordinary variation between people and days.

## What actually changes your needs

Exercise increases fluid loss through sweat, and the amount varies widely between people, intensity, and conditions — two people doing the same workout can lose very different amounts. Hot or dry climates increase losses even without any exercise at all. Body size matters too — a simple guide many people use is a range proportional to bodyweight, adjusted upward on hard training days or hot weather, rather than a flat number applied to everyone regardless of circumstances.

## Practical signals worth trusting

Thirst is a reasonable guide for most healthy adults doing normal daily activity — drink when thirsty rather than forcing water on a rigid schedule out of habit. Urine color is another useful, low-effort check: pale yellow generally suggests adequate hydration, while consistently dark urine suggests it is time to drink more throughout the day. Neither signal needs to be tracked obsessively or logged in detail to be useful.

## A useful next step

For the next few days, skip counting glasses and instead check your urine color once or twice a day and pay attention to how thirsty you feel during and after activity. Use those two simple signals to adjust your intake naturally, instead of chasing a fixed number that was never really about you.
