Of all the supplements sold for training, creatine and protein powder have by far the most consistent research behind them. Neither is magic, and neither replaces food or training, but both do something specific and well-documented enough to be worth understanding on their own terms.

## What protein powder actually is

Protein powder — commonly whey, casein, egg, or a plant blend — is simply a concentrated, convenient protein source, not a fundamentally different substance from the protein in chicken or lentils. Whey digests quickly and is a complete protein (it supplies all nine essential amino acids), which is why it's commonly used around training; casein digests slowly, which is why some people prefer it before bed. Most healthy adults training regularly do well with roughly 1.4–2.0 grams of protein per kilogram of bodyweight per day from all sources combined — food included — and a shake is only useful for closing the gap between what your meals provide and that target, not a replacement for eating enough protein overall.

## What creatine actually does

Creatine is stored mostly in muscle as phosphocreatine, where it helps regenerate ATP — the immediate energy currency your muscles use during short, hard efforts like a heavy set or a sprint. Supplementing raises those stores, which is associated with modest but real improvements in high-intensity training capacity and, over time, in strength and lean mass gained from a given training program. It's one of the most-researched supplements in sports nutrition, and large reviews have consistently found it safe for healthy adults at standard doses.

## Realistic dosing for each

For creatine, a simple approach is 3–5 grams of creatine monohydrate daily, taken consistently — a "loading phase" of 20 grams a day for 5–7 days saturates muscle stores faster, but the same saturation happens on its own within about a month at the lower daily dose, so loading is a convenience choice, not a requirement. For protein powder, one to two scoops (roughly 25–50 grams) placed wherever it's most convenient in your day — after training, with a meal light on protein, or as a snack — is a reasonable default; timing relative to a workout matters far less than hitting your total daily protein target.

## Our take: who benefits most, and who can skip both

Creatine's benefit is largest for people doing repeated high-intensity efforts — lifting, sprinting, team sports — and is far less relevant if your training is mostly low-intensity cardio or mobility work. Protein powder is most useful for people who genuinely struggle to hit their protein target through meals alone, whether from appetite, schedule, or food preference — if you're already meeting your target with food, a shake adds convenience, not extra results. Both are optional, not required: years of consistent training, sleep, and adequate food intake will outperform either supplement on its own, and if you have kidney, liver, or other relevant health conditions, check with a doctor before adding either one, since dosing guidance for the general population doesn't automatically apply to every individual.
