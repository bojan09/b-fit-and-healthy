Fitness and nutrition marketing is full of confident promises. Most people know, in general, to be skeptical — but specific claims can still be persuasive in the moment. Having a short mental checklist makes it easier to pause and evaluate before believing or buying.

## Miracle timelines and single-product solutions

Be cautious of any claim promising dramatic results in an unusually short time, especially when it's tied to one product, plan, or exercise. Meaningful changes in strength, body composition, or health markers generally take consistent effort over weeks and months, and no single item realistically replaces that broader pattern.

## Before-and-after extremes and "secret" methods

Dramatic before-and-after photos can be shaped by lighting, posing, hydration, and selective timing rather than the product being sold. Similarly, language suggesting a "secret" the industry doesn't want you to know is a common persuasion tactic — legitimate, well-studied approaches to training and nutrition are publicly available and not gatekept.

## Questions to ask before trusting a claim

Who is making this claim, and do they benefit financially from you believing it? Is there research behind it, and does that research actually match what's being promised? Would a qualified professional — a doctor, dietitian, or certified trainer — describe it the same way? If a claim can't hold up under these questions, it's worth treating with real caution.

## A practical next step

The next time you see a compelling fitness or nutrition claim, pause and run it through these questions before acting on it. Treating strong claims as a starting point for research, rather than as settled fact, will serve you better than either blind trust or blanket dismissal.
