Lunges, step-ups, split squats, and running all ask one leg to support the whole body for a moment. That job falls mostly to the muscles around the hip, which have to control the pelvis and thigh in three directions at once, not just bend and straighten the knee.

## The hip's balancing act

The glutes, particularly the muscles on the side of the hip, work to keep the pelvis level when one foot is on the ground. The hamstrings help control the hip and knee together, especially as the body decelerates into a lunge or step-down. Together, this group resists the leg collapsing inward or the pelvis dropping to one side.

## Why knees cave in

A knee that drifts inward during a lunge or squat is often described as a "weak" muscle, but it is more accurate to think of it as a control issue. When the hip muscles cannot manage the load fast enough, the thigh rotates inward and the knee follows. This is common when the movement is new, fatigue sets in, or the pace is faster than the person can currently stabilize.

## Building single-leg control

Control develops through practice at a manageable pace before adding load or speed. Slow, supported single-leg movements, like a split squat with a hand on a rail, let the hip muscles learn the pattern without being overwhelmed. Cueing the knee to track roughly over the middle toes, rather than forcing a rigid line, gives the body something concrete to aim for.

## What to try next

Pick one single-leg exercise, such as a step-up or split squat, and slow down the descent for a few sets, paying attention to whether the knee stays reasonably aligned with the foot. If it consistently caves regardless of pace or load, reducing the range of motion or the height of the step for a few weeks is a reasonable adjustment. Ongoing knee pain or instability is worth having assessed by a physiotherapist rather than working around indefinitely.
