"How many calories should I eat?" has a rough, estimable answer, even though no formula can be exact for an individual on a given day. This walkthrough covers the two steps: estimating what your body burns at rest, then adjusting for your activity and your goal.

## Step one: estimate your resting needs

Your basal metabolic rate (BMR) is roughly how many calories your body burns just to stay alive at rest — breathing, circulating blood, maintaining organ function. A commonly used estimate (the Harris-Benedict equation) is:

- **Men:** 66 + (13.7 × weight in kg) + (5 × height in cm) − (6.8 × age in years)
- **Women:** 655 + (9.6 × weight in kg) + (1.8 × height in cm) − (4.7 × age in years)

For example, an 80 kg, 180 cm, 25-year-old man would calculate: 66 + (13.7 × 80) + (5 × 180) − (6.8 × 25) ≈ 1,892 calories. This is only a starting estimate — individual metabolisms vary by a meaningful margin around any formula, so treat the number as a reasonable first guess, not a lab-verified figure.

## Step two: add your activity level

BMR alone doesn't account for movement, so multiply it by an activity factor to estimate your total daily energy expenditure (TDEE):

- **×1.3** — mostly sedentary (desk-based work, minimal movement through the day)
- **×1.55** — on your feet for much of the day (retail, teaching, similar roles)
- **×1.65** — regular structured exercise, roughly 2 hours a day of training or cycling
- **×1.80** — physically demanding work or high-volume athletic training (around 4 hours a day)

Using the example above: 1,892 × 1.3 ≈ 2,460 calories to maintain current weight at a low activity level. That total is the number to adjust from, not the BMR figure alone.

## Step three: adjust for your goal

For gradual fat loss, subtract roughly 500–750 calories from your maintenance total, which tends to produce about 0.5 kg of loss per week. For gradual weight gain, add the same range instead. Both directions work better as a range than a fixed daily number — your body adapts over time, so revisit the calculation every few weeks rather than treating the first result as permanent.

## Our take: what the formula can't tell you

Every version of this calculation is a population-average estimate, and individual results can land meaningfully above or below it — which is exactly why "if the scale hasn't moved in a month, check for a hidden source of extra calories" (sauces, drinks, alcohol, oversized portions) before assuming the math is wrong. Treat the number from this calculation as a starting point to test against your own results over 3–4 weeks, then adjust up or down based on what actually happens, rather than trusting the formula over your own tracked data.
