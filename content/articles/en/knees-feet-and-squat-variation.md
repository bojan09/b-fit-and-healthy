Few exercises attract as much conflicting advice as the squat. One common rule — "knees should never go past your toes" — is repeated so often it sounds like biomechanical law. In reality, it is an oversimplification that ignores how much normal variation exists in human hip and ankle structure.

## Why one stance doesn't fit everyone

Hip socket depth, femur length, and the angle at which the thigh bone meets the pelvis all differ between individuals. Someone with a deeper hip socket or longer femurs may need a wider stance and more forward knee travel to squat comfortably, while someone else may naturally sit into a narrower, more upright position. Neither pattern is inherently wrong; they reflect the person's own skeleton.

## The role of ankle mobility

Limited ankle mobility restricts how far the shin can travel forward, which changes how the rest of the body must compensate — often through a more forward torso lean or a wider stance with toes turned out. This isn't a flaw to eliminate but a starting point to work with, whether through gradual mobility work or by choosing a stance that suits the current range available.

## Finding a stance that works for you

A useful approach is to experiment with foot width and toe angle across a few sessions, paying attention to where the knees track comfortably over the feet and where depth feels stable rather than strained. Some discomfort from unfamiliarity is normal early on; sharp or persistent joint pain is not, and is a signal to adjust the setup or load rather than push through it.

## A practical next step

At your next lower-body session, try two or three small adjustments — slightly wider stance, slightly more toe-out, or a partial range of motion — and notice which combination lets you keep a stable spine and comfortable knee tracking. Build your working squat pattern around what your own hips and ankles allow, not a single rule applied to every body.
