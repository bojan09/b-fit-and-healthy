Macronutrients — protein, carbohydrate, and fat — get most of the attention because they supply calories. Micronutrients, the vitamins and minerals in your food, don't provide energy directly, but they're what let your body actually use that energy: building blood cells, supporting immune function, and keeping bones, nerves, and hormones working properly.

## Vitamins versus minerals

Vitamins are organic compounds your body needs in small amounts for normal function; most have to come from food, though vitamin D can also be made through sun exposure and a couple of B-vitamins can be produced by gut bacteria. Minerals are inorganic elements — calcium, iron, potassium, zinc, and others — that the body can't manufacture at all, so every bit has to come from what you eat or drink. No single food contains every vitamin and mineral in the right amount, which is the practical reason variety matters more than any one "superfood."

## Why variety does more than any single supplement

Because different food groups concentrate different micronutrients — citrus and peppers for vitamin C, leafy greens and dairy for calcium, meat and legumes for iron and zinc — eating a genuinely varied diet across the week covers far more ground than fixating on one nutrient in isolation. This is also why fortified foods exist: bread and cereal are often fortified with specific vitamins precisely because a narrow diet tends to fall short on them.

## Where gaps most commonly show up

Certain patterns raise the odds of a shortfall: diets that exclude entire food groups (very low fruit and vegetable intake, or fully plant-based diets without attention to B12), very restrictive calorie intakes, and some life stages such as pregnancy or older age. None of these mean you definitely have a deficiency — they just mean it's worth paying more deliberate attention to variety, and worth a conversation with a doctor or dietitian if you suspect an actual gap rather than guessing with supplements.

## A practical next step

Instead of trying to memorize which food contains which micronutrient, aim for color and category variety across a week: different vegetables, a couple of fruit choices, a mix of protein sources, and some whole grains or legumes. If you eat a similar handful of foods on repeat, that's a more useful signal to diversify than any specific nutrient number — and if you have a genuine concern about a deficiency, a blood test through your doctor is more informative than guessing.
