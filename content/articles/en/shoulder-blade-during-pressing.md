Bench presses, overhead presses, and push-ups all involve more than the arms and chest. The shoulder blade, or scapula, glides along the rib cage to support the arm through its range of motion. Many lifters are told to keep it "pinned" the whole time, but that cue is often misunderstood.

## Why the shoulder blade needs to move

The scapula naturally rotates and shifts as the arm rises overhead or extends during a press. This movement helps the shoulder joint stay in a safe, efficient position. Locking the shoulder blade completely still can restrict range of motion and shift stress onto structures that are not built to absorb it.

## Where the "shoulders down and back" cue came from

This cue was popularized as a way to stop lifters from rounding forward and shrugging excessively under load, which can strain the neck and upper traps. It is useful as a starting reference, especially on a flat bench. But treating it as a rigid rule for every press, every rep, ignores that the scapula is designed to rotate upward once the arm passes shoulder height, such as during an overhead press.

## Finding a comfortable, functional position

A practical approach is to set the shoulder blades gently back and down before the first rep, mainly to create a stable base on the bench, then let them move naturally as the weight travels. If a press causes pinching, sharp pain, or numbness, that is different from normal muscular effort and worth easing off rather than pushing through.

## What to do at your next session

Start your pressing sets by rolling the shoulders back once to settle into position, then focus on control through the full range rather than trying to keep the shoulder blades frozen. If a particular pressing angle consistently feels uncomfortable, try adjusting bar path, grip width, or bench angle before assuming more mobility work is the fix. Persistent or worsening shoulder pain is worth discussing with a physiotherapist or sports medicine professional.
