Fiber is the part of plant food your body cannot fully digest, and that is exactly why it matters. It moves through the digestive tract, feeds gut bacteria, slows the rise in blood sugar after meals, and adds bulk that helps you feel full. Unlike most nutrients, its value comes partly from what it does not do — it passes through largely intact, shaping the process along the way rather than being absorbed for energy.

## What fiber is actually doing

Soluble fiber, found in oats, beans, and fruit, forms a gel-like substance that can steady digestion and blunt blood sugar spikes after a meal. Insoluble fiber, found in whole grains, vegetable skins, and nuts, adds bulk that keeps things moving through the gut at a steady pace. Most whole foods contain a mix of both types, so you rarely need to track them separately — eating a variety of plant foods naturally covers both.

## A realistic daily target

Many health guidelines point to roughly 25–30 grams of fiber per day for adults, though the exact figure varies with body size, sex, and typical calorie intake. Most people eating a diet of mostly refined grains, packaged snacks, and few vegetables fall well below that number, often without realizing it. Treat the target as a useful general direction rather than a number to chase precisely every single day.

## Increase intake gradually

Jumping from a low-fiber diet to a very high one quickly often causes bloating, gas, or general digestive discomfort, which can make the whole change feel like a mistake. A steadier approach is to add one extra source every few days — a piece of fruit, a spoonful of beans stirred into a meal, or swapping white rice for a whole grain — and to increase water intake alongside it, since fiber needs fluid to move through the gut comfortably.

## A useful next step

Pick one meal today that currently has little or no fiber and add a single easy source: a side of vegetables, a handful of berries, or a swap to whole grain bread instead of white. Build from that one change over the coming weeks rather than trying to overhaul every meal at once.
