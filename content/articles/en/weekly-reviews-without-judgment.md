A weekly review is a short pause to look at what actually happened, not a performance report to grade yourself against. Done well, it takes ten minutes and tells you more than any amount of guilt would.

## Why judgment gets in the way

Harsh self-criticism feels productive in the moment, but it tends to make people avoid looking at their own data. If reviewing the week means confronting shame, most people eventually stop reviewing at all, and the habit of checking in disappears along with the guilt they were trying to avoid. A neutral, observational tone keeps the habit of checking in alive, which matters more than any single week's result — you can only improve a pattern you are willing to look at.

## Questions worth asking

Keep it simple: what did I actually do this week, what got in the way on the days I missed, and did the plan itself fit the week I had. These questions describe reality instead of scoring it, which makes them easier to answer honestly. Notice patterns — a repeated obstacle, like evenings that always run late or a lunch that always gets skipped, is more useful information than a single missed session, because it points to something fixable in the schedule rather than a personal failing.

## Turn observations into small adjustments

The point of the review is to inform next week, not to relitigate this one. If mornings consistently work better than evenings, shift the plan instead of forcing the same slot to work again. If a goal was unrealistic for your schedule, scale it down rather than trying harder with the same setup that already failed twice. Small, steady adjustments compound over months; regret does not, and it rarely changes what happens next week anyway.

## Do a two-minute version tonight

Answer just two questions in a notes app: what worked this week, and what is one small change for next week. No scoring, no explanations required — just enough to carry forward.
