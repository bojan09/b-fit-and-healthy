A repetition, or "rep," is one complete movement of an exercise. A "set" is a group of repetitions performed without stopping to rest. Together they describe how much work is done, but the number alone says little without knowing how hard each rep felt.

## What reps and sets are measuring

Reps and sets are a way of counting volume, not a guarantee of results by themselves. Ten reps performed with a weight that feels easy is a very different stimulus than ten reps performed with a weight that feels genuinely challenging by the last few. The count matters, but so does the load and how close to fatigue the set gets you.

## Effort scales, in plain terms

Rate of Perceived Effort (RPE) is a simple way to describe how hard a set felt, usually on a 1–10 scale, where 10 means no more reps were possible with good form. A set performed at RPE 7 or 8 — leaving a couple of reps in reserve — is a common, sustainable target for most training. This scale is subjective and improves with practice, but it gives useful information that a rep count alone cannot.

## Why "3x10" isn't a universal law

Three sets of ten is a popular default, not a scientifically fixed prescription. Lower reps with heavier loads tend to emphasize strength and skill with a movement; higher reps with lighter loads can still build muscle and endurance when the effort is genuinely challenging. The right combination depends on the goal, the exercise, and how the body responds — not on a number that shows up in every generic program.

## A useful next step

For your next workout, rate the last set of each exercise on the 1–10 effort scale after finishing it. Over a few sessions, you will start to see whether your sets are consistently too easy, appropriately challenging, or pushed further than intended.
