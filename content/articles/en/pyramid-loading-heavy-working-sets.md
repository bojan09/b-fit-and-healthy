This is a classic ramping structure associated with old-school bodybuilders who trained a muscle group to one or two genuinely hard sets rather than many moderate ones. It's built around a simple idea: use several warm-up sets to prepare the joint and rehearse the movement, then spend your hardest effort on just one or two working sets instead of spreading fatigue across five or six.

## The structure

- **Set 0 — pre-warm-up:** roughly 20 easy reps with light or no load, purely to get blood into the area and rehearse the movement pattern.
- **Set 1 — warm-up:** about 15 reps at a light-to-moderate load.
- **Set 2 — warm-up:** about 12 reps, load increased again.
- **Set 3 — working set:** about 10 reps, slow and controlled, at a load that is genuinely challenging by the last couple of reps.
- **Set 4 — working set:** about 8 reps, heavier again, still slow and controlled.

Do this for 4–5 exercises per session, one muscle group at a time. Each working set should feel hard by the final reps, but every rep — including the last — stays slow and under control. This is not a format for grinding out sloppy, momentum-driven reps.

## Why the ramp matters

The point of the warm-up sets isn't just raising body temperature — it's rehearsing the bar path or joint pattern at low stakes so that by the time you reach a genuinely heavy set, your form is already dialed in and your joints have had a chance to adjust to the load gradually rather than jumping straight into a hard set cold.

## Our take: what the original method changes here, and why it matters

This structure is commonly attributed to the bodybuilder Dorian Yates, but his actual approach also included assisted forced reps and training each muscle group to true failure only once every 4–5 days with long recovery windows — details that don't translate safely into a general-audience guide without in-person coaching and a genuine base of training experience. What's presented here is a simplified ramping structure inspired by that idea, not a reproduction of it, and it's better thought of as "prepare thoroughly, then work hard on a couple of sets" rather than a true failure-training protocol.

Treat the working sets as hard, not maximal — stop each set with a rep or two still in the tank rather than pushing to a true failure point, especially if you're newer to structured training. This ramping style is best suited to lifts you already know well technically; save it for a compound movement you can perform safely under fatigue, and give the muscle group involved at least 48 hours before training it this hard again.
