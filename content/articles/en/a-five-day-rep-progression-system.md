Most people plateau on bodyweight numbers because they do the same push-up or pull-up set day after day. This five-day format works one exercise from five different angles across a week, which gives the nervous system, the muscle, and your motivation a different stimulus each day instead of repeating the same effort. It works for push-ups, pull-ups, sit-ups, or squats — pick one movement per week and log every number so you know what to beat next time.

## Day one: max reps in one set

Warm up, then do a single set to a genuine maximum, followed by four more sets of that same movement with 90 seconds of rest between them. Write down every number. This day tells you your current ceiling and gives you a baseline to chase the following week — the numbers from later sets are usually lower than the first, and that is expected, not a failure.

## Day two: the pyramid

Do 1 rep, rest 10 seconds, do 2 reps, rest 10 seconds, do 3, and keep climbing by one rep each round with a short rest between. Once you can no longer add a rep, come back down the same way: one fewer rep each round until you reach 1. This accumulates a large amount of volume in short bursts and teaches you to keep working through rising fatigue.

## Day three: three variations, self-set reps

Pick three related variations of the movement — for push-ups that could be a close-hand version, a standard version, and a wide-hand version. Do three sets of each variation, nine sets total, resting 60 seconds between sets. You choose the rep count per set: something that feels sustainable but not trivial, never fewer than 3 reps. The following week, try to add one rep to each set; if a five-rep jump isn't realistic, hold steady at your current number until it feels ready to rise again.

## Day four: nine sets of your weakest variation

Pick whichever variation from day three gave you the most trouble. Do nine sets of it at a rep count you can actually complete with control — even if that's only 1 or 2 reps per set — resting 60 seconds between sets. This day is deliberately about the version you're worst at, since that's usually where the biggest long-term gains are hiding.

## Day five: repeat your hardest day

Look back at the week and repeat whichever of the four days felt the toughest. This isn't about punishing yourself — it's a second exposure to the stimulus that challenged you most, which tends to be where adaptation is still incomplete.

## Our take: who this suits and who should skip it

This format rewards people who already own solid technique in the base movement and want a structured way to chase higher numbers — it is not a beginner's introduction to push-ups or pull-ups. If you can't yet do the movement with full control for a handful of reps, spend a few weeks on the standard version first; day four in particular asks you to grind through a weak variation for nine sets, which only makes sense once your joints already tolerate the movement well. Because the volume adds up quickly across a week, treat one "hardest day" repeat as your ceiling — don't stack this format on top of a full strength program for the same muscle group without a rest day somewhere in between, and stop the session early if a joint aches rather than a muscle merely burning.
